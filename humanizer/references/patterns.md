# Patterns that give away AI writing

Based on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (WikiProject AI Cleanup). Each entry has what it looks like and how to fix it. The page itself stresses that none of these prove text is AI-written, and that removing them without fixing the underlying vagueness only hides the problem.

## 1. Content

### 1.1 Undue emphasis on significance, legacy and broader trends
Looks like: "marks a pivotal moment," "a key turning point," "left an indelible mark," "sets the stage for," "reflects the evolving landscape," "deeply rooted," "a focal point," "plays a vital role," "contributes to the broader understanding of."
Fix: delete it, or replace it with the concrete consequence. "The 2019 survey changed national policy" beats "the survey marked a pivotal moment in the evolving landscape of malaria control." Better still: "After the 2019 survey, the NMEP switched to dual-AI nets."

### 1.2 Undue emphasis on notability and media coverage
Looks like: listing every newspaper mention, interview, award or "has been featured in" to make a subject seem important.
Fix: keep only mentions that carry information the reader needs.

### 1.3 Superficial analysis via -ing phrases
Looks like: ", highlighting the importance of...", ", underscoring the need for...", ", reflecting a broader commitment to...", ", showcasing its versatility."
Fix: cut the tail. If there's a real implication, give it its own sentence with evidence.

### 1.4 Promotional, advertisement-like language
Looks like: vibrant, rich (heritage/history/tapestry), breathtaking, stunning, nestled, renowned, world-class, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, seamless, unparalleled, boasts, a must-visit, a hidden gem, stands as a testament.
Fix: neutral description with facts. "The lab has two MinION devices and a GridION" instead of "boasts state-of-the-art sequencing capabilities."

### 1.5 Editorializing and didactic disclaimers
Looks like: "It's important to note," "It's worth mentioning," "It should be emphasized," "Notably," "Interestingly," "Crucially," "It's essential to remember."
Fix: delete the lead-in and state the point.

### 1.6 Section summaries that restate
Looks like: a final sentence or paragraph opening with "In summary," "In conclusion," "Overall," "Ultimately," "In essence," "To sum up," that repeats what came before.
Fix: end on the last substantive point. In formal documents that need a conclusion, the conclusion should add a judgment or implication, not a recap.

### 1.7 Outline-style "challenges and future prospects" endings
Looks like: "Despite these achievements, X faces several challenges..." followed by generic issues, then "Looking ahead, X is poised to..."
Fix: remove unless there are specific, sourced challenges. Never write "poised to," "is set to," "the future looks bright."

### 1.8 Vague attribution and weasel words
Looks like: "Experts argue," "Researchers have noted," "Studies suggest," "Critics point out," "It is widely believed," "Many consider."
Fix: name who said it and cite it, or drop the claim.

## 2. Language and grammar

### 2.1 High density of AI vocabulary
See the list in section 7. One of these words on its own is fine. Several in a paragraph is the tell.

### 2.2 Copula avoidance
Looks like: "serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "acts as," "represents," "boasts," "features," "offers" in place of "is" or "has."
Fix: "The clinic is the district's only referral centre," not "The clinic serves as the district's sole referral hub."

### 2.3 Negative parallelisms
Looks like: "It's not X, it's Y." "This isn't just a tool, it's a movement." "Not only A but also B." "No X. No Y. Just Z." "It's less about X and more about Y."
Fix: state the positive claim directly. "The tool also tracks resistance markers."

### 2.4 Rule of three
Looks like: triplets everywhere. "Efficient, scalable, and robust." "Clarity, consistency, and impact." Three examples, three benefits, three adjectives, paragraph after paragraph.
Fix: use the number of items the content actually has. Vary it. Often one precise word beats three loose ones.

### 2.5 Elegant variation (synonym cycling)
Looks like: "Kumasi... the city... the Garden City... the urban centre... the metropolis" within a few sentences.
Fix: repeat the clearest noun, or use a pronoun.

### 2.6 False ranges
Looks like: "from smallholder farms to global markets," "from ancient traditions to modern innovations," where the two ends don't define a real scale.
Fix: list what's actually covered, or drop it.

### 2.7 Hedge stacking and filler transitions
Looks like: "may potentially," "could possibly indicate," "it can be argued that it might"; sentences chained with "Additionally," "Moreover," "Furthermore," "In addition," "Consequently."
Fix: one hedge where uncertainty is real. Let sentences connect through their content; use plain "also," "but," "so" when a connector is needed.

### 2.8 Uniform rhythm
Looks like: every sentence roughly the same length, every paragraph the same shape (claim, elaboration, significance).
Fix: mix short and long sentences. Let some paragraphs be one line.

## 3. Style and formatting

### 3.1 Em dashes
Looks like: "The results were clear — resistance had spread." Often with spaces around the dash, often used for dramatic emphasis or to tack on a clause. The Wikipedia page notes a July 2026 study finding Claude in particular uses them more than professional writers.
Fix: none at all. Use a comma, colon, semicolon, parentheses or a full stop. En dashes stay for ranges (pages 12–18, 2019–2024).

### 3.2 Title Case Headings
Fix: sentence case. "Study design and sampling," not "Study Design And Sampling."

### 3.3 Excessive boldface
Looks like: key terms bolded throughout running text, bolded phrases followed by a sentence restating them.
Fix: no bold in prose. Let sentence structure carry emphasis.

### 3.4 Inline-header vertical lists
Looks like:
- **Efficiency:** The pipeline is efficient because...
- **Scalability:** It scales to...
Fix: write it as a paragraph, or use a plain list without the bold label when the content is genuinely list-like.

### 3.5 Emojis
Looks like: emojis in headings, bullet points, or formal text.
Fix: remove unless explicitly requested.

### 3.6 Over-structuring
Looks like: headings on a 200-word answer, bullet points for reasoning, tables nobody needed, horizontal rules between every section.
Fix: paragraphs by default. Structure only when it helps the reader find things.

### 3.7 Curly vs straight quotes
Looks like: a mix of ‘curly’ and 'straight' quotes or apostrophes, or curly quotes in a context that otherwise uses straight ones.
Fix: be consistent; straight quotes by default.

## 4. Communication aimed at the user

### 4.1 Collaborative chatbot filler
Looks like: "Certainly!", "Of course!", "Absolutely!", "Great question!", "I'd be happy to help," "Here's a revised version," "I hope this helps!", "Let me know if you'd like any changes," "Feel free to reach out."
Fix: delete. In emails Stephen sends, a normal sign-off like "Thanks" or "Best regards" is fine; the chatbot-flavoured ones aren't.

### 4.2 Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers and gap-filling speculation
Looks like: "As of my last update," "While specific details are limited," "Information on this is not widely available, but it is likely that..."
Fix: remove. If a fact is unknown, say plainly that it's unknown or go and find it.

### 4.3 Refusal and AI-identity text
Looks like: "As an AI language model, I cannot..."
Fix: never in final text.

### 4.4 Placeholders and template phrases
Looks like: [Your Name], [Institution], [insert statistic], "Dear [Recipient]," "[Date]".
Fix: fill them from what's known, or ask Stephen before delivering.

### 4.5 Sycophancy
Looks like: "This is a fantastic draft!", "Your research is truly groundbreaking," "What a thoughtful question."
Fix: remove. Give honest, specific feedback instead.

## 5. Markup and citations

### 5.1 Markdown or markup in the wrong place
Looks like: **asterisks** or ## hashes showing up in a Word document, email or form field that doesn't render them; broken formatting.
Fix: format for the destination.

### 5.2 Chatbot artifacts
Looks like: "turn0search0," "【4†source】," "oaicite," ":contentReference," links ending "?utm_source=chatgpt.com."
Fix: strip them.

### 5.3 Fabricated or broken citations
Looks like: plausible references that don't exist, wrong DOIs or ISBNs, real papers cited for claims they don't make, dead links, invented page numbers or quotes.
Fix: only cite what's been verified. If a citation can't be checked, flag it for Stephen rather than guessing. This matters most in his thesis, proposals and fellowship applications.

## 6. Miscellaneous

### 6.1 Sudden style shift
Looks like: one section in a noticeably different voice, vocabulary or polish from the rest of the document.
Fix: when editing part of Stephen's document, match the voice of the surrounding text.

### 6.2 Overlong explanations of small things
Looks like: a paragraph where a line would do; explaining edits at length.
Fix: keep notes and summaries short.

### 6.3 Letter format where it doesn't belong
Looks like: "Dear reader," salutations or sign-offs on text that isn't a letter.
Fix: remove.

## 7. Stock vocabulary list

Avoid these, especially in combination. Pick the plain word instead.

delve, dive into, tapestry, testament, intricate, intricacies, underscore, highlight (as a verb for "show"), foster, showcase, pivotal, crucial, vital, paramount, landscape, realm, sphere, robust, leverage, harness, navigate, multifaceted, nuanced, holistic, comprehensive, meticulous, meticulously, seamless, seamlessly, vibrant, rich, enduring, profound, invaluable, garner, bolster, elevate, empower, embark, unlock, unveil, spearhead, streamline, align with, resonate, commendable, noteworthy, notably, groundbreaking, transformative, cutting-edge, game-changer, ever-evolving, dynamic, synergy, paradigm, interplay, cornerstone, beacon, catalyst, stands as, serves as, boasts, additionally, moreover, furthermore, in today's world, in the realm of, a myriad of, plethora, it is worth noting, at its core, in essence

Plain alternatives: show, use, important, area, field, strong, detailed, careful, help, support, build, start, explore, look at, examine, also, and, but, so.

## 8. Before and after examples

Fellowship statement
Before: "My research stands as a testament to my unwavering commitment to tackling malaria — a disease that continues to shape the landscape of public health in Ghana. Through meticulous genomic analysis, I aim to foster a deeper understanding of transmission dynamics, paving the way for innovative, data-driven, and impactful interventions."
After: "I study how Plasmodium falciparum moves between communities in Ghana. My PhD uses parasite genomes to measure genetic connectivity between sites, which tells control programmes where transmission is linked and where interventions in one district are likely to affect another."

Email
Before: "I hope this email finds you well! I wanted to reach out to kindly follow up on my previous message regarding the sequencing run. It's not just about the timeline — it's about ensuring data quality. Please let me know at your earliest convenience. Thank you so much!"
After: "Hi Dr Mensah, following up on my message from Tuesday about the sequencing run. Could you confirm whether the flow cells arrived? I'd like to load the library before Friday so it doesn't sit in the freezer. Thanks, Stephen"

Methods paragraph
Before: "Additionally, robust quality control was meticulously performed, serving as a crucial step to ensure the reliability of downstream analyses. Moreover, this approach highlights the importance of data integrity in genomic research."
After: "Reads were trimmed with fastp (Q20, minimum length 50 bp) and samples with under 80% of amplicons at 10x depth were excluded."
(If the actual thresholds aren't known, ask; don't invent them.)
